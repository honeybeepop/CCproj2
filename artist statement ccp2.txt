I chose to create something for this project with a large amount of anger and fear behind it, because I wanted it to be both vulnerable and guarded. I based this piece off of my reactions and emotions over time regarding the rape of Jane Doe at Cornell University in October of 2024. This piece is aptly named Fury of Jane. This subject, being the portrayal of victims of assault and societal handling of acts of violence specifically towards women, holds a heavy weight on every woman I know. If it wasn’t you, then it was someone you know. I wanted to portray the crushing feeling of fear and anger experienced as a woman within the confines of how we are often seen—as animals. Prey. “Jane Doe”, a placeholder name for unidentified or anonymous women, has been used for centuries. I chose to use deer as a placeholder for both female victims, the doe in the center of the frame, as well as a stag in the background, highlighting both the underreported nature of male victims of sexual assaults, as well as creating a sense of unease in the viewer, unsure whether the stag is protecting or harming the doe. This reflects specifically to the experience of women, constantly evaluating whether or not the men they know are a threat or a guardian. More often than not, though, they merely stand to the side, never choosing to help nor harm. Jane Doe, the doe I’ve represented, is surrounded by human skeletons—seven of them to be exact—and harbors anatomy more similar to that of a wolf. Does are often defenseless in their herd, but that is not the case in victims of assault, hence why Jane Doe bares her carnivorous fangs and takes a defensive stance throughout the piece. As for the coding aspect of my project, I chose to make the blood upon the stag’s antlers randomized, as predators of a human nature are unpredictable and, often, hard to pick out of a crowd. Further randomization is within the turning of Jane Doe, whether she looks to the side, or directly at the viewer. Lastly for my choices of randomization, I chose to make the appearance of the scope work within a random loop, as many perpetrators of assault are either never caught or walk away from allegations scott-free. As such, whether or not the scope is present above the stag is random. As for my choices in patterning, I chose to find patterns in human anatomy, within the skeletons littering the ground, as well as the iconic fawn spotting pattern. Fawns often lose their spots as they age, so I wanted to keep Jane Doe’s spots prominent as a way to highlight the nature of sexual assaults—an act of power and violence, often committed as those seen as weak or powerless, like a child. Lastly, I wanted to include the subtle patterning of the forest in the background, as I personally find forests comforting and see them as a space where I am able to relax because I can easily be aware of my surroundings in the soothing noises of the forest.
